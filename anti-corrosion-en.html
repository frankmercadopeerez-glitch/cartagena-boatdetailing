Marine corrosion in the Caribbean

Why Marine Metals Need Extra Care in Cartagena

Salt spray, heat, humidity and limited ventilation keep marine metals exposed to moisture and chloride deposits. In engine rooms, bilges and exterior fittings, that combination can accelerate staining, pitting and coating failure when surfaces are not cleaned and protected correctly.

Corrosion is not always cosmetic. Deterioration around mixed-metal connections, fasteners, supports and electrical equipment can affect reliability and lead to more extensive repairs. A documented inspection helps identify the source before specifying cleaning, surface preparation, anodes or protective coatings.

Three Types of Corrosion We Look For

Saltwater Corrosion

Salt deposits can remain in pores and crevices, weakening protective films and promoting staining or pitting on exposed metal surfaces.

Galvanic Corrosion

This can occur when dissimilar metals are electrically connected in a conductive saltwater environment. The less noble metal may deteriorate unless the system is correctly isolated and protected.

Heat and Moisture Corrosion

Warm, humid compartments can trap salt-laden moisture around exhaust elbows, engine mounts and poorly ventilated surfaces, increasing the need for cleaning and coating maintenance.

Areas and Components We Can Protect

The exact scope is defined after inspection and may include more than the engine room, depending on access and the boat's condition.

Engine Room

Engines, exhaust elbows, mounts, metal hose fittings, electrical panels and exposed steel or aluminum surfaces in the propulsion compartment.

Shaft and Propeller

The propulsion shaft, stern gear, shaft seal and propeller are checked for galvanic exposure and coating or passivation needs. Submerged work normally requires a haul-out.

Sacrificial Anode System

Inspection of condition, placement and compatibility of zinc or aluminum anodes. Replacement is recommended when the inspection shows it is needed.

Rudder and Steering Hardware

Rudders, steering hardware and fittings are checked for visible corrosion, coating damage and areas where dissimilar metals meet.

Exterior Metalwork

Arches, rails, rod holders, anchor lockers, ladders and exterior stainless-steel or aluminum hardware can be cleaned, polished or passivated according to the material.

Bilge and Internal Structure

Bilges and internal structures can collect saltwater, freshwater and contaminants. The proposed work may include degreasing, rust removal and a compatible protective coating.

Method

Anti-Corrosion Treatment Process

A seven-step workflow adapted to the metal, access and condition.

1

Inspection and Diagnosis

Visual assessment of accessible components and photographic documentation of the initial condition. We identify visible rust, pitting, coating failure and possible galvanic exposure.

2

Marine-Safe Degreasing

Oil, grease, fuel residue and surface contaminants are removed with products compatible with the work area. Clean preparation is essential for coating adhesion.

3

Mechanical Rust Removal

Active corrosion and loose coatings are removed with suitable hand or power tools. The level of surface preparation is set according to the selected coating system and safe access.

4

Rust Conversion Where Appropriate

A compatible rust converter may be used on residual oxidation when the substrate and coating specification call for it. It is not applied as a substitute for proper preparation.

5

Marine Anti-Corrosion Primer

A compatible marine primer is applied to the prepared metal. Product selection depends on the substrate, operating temperature, exposure and finish system.

6

Protective Topcoat System

The specified protective finish is applied in the number of coats and curing intervals required by the selected product. Engine-room temperature and chemical exposure are considered.

7

Anode Review and Final Inspection

We review accessible sacrificial anodes, recommend replacement where appropriate and complete a final visual inspection with before-and-after documentation of the agreed work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Anti-Corrosion Treatment Questions

How often should a boat receive anti-corrosion treatment in Cartagena?
The correct interval depends on the metals, coating system, ventilation, use and current condition. We recommend a periodic inspection and define the maintenance cycle after evaluating the boat.
How can I tell whether my boat has active galvanic corrosion?
Possible warning signs include unusually rapid anode wear, localized pitting near electrical connections and deterioration where different metals meet. A technical inspection is needed to confirm the cause and define the repair.
Are zinc and aluminum anodes interchangeable?
No. The correct sacrificial anode depends on the water, the protected metal and the boat's existing system. We inspect the installation before recommending zinc or aluminum anodes.
Does anti-corrosion treatment require hauling the boat out?
It depends on the components involved. Engine rooms and many topside structures can be treated at the dock, while submerged components such as shafts, propellers and rudders normally require a haul-out.
Does stainless steel also need corrosion care?
Yes. Stainless steel is corrosion resistant, not corrosion proof. Salt deposits, crevices and poor ventilation can lead to staining or pitting, so the correct cleaning and passivation method is selected after inspection.
What is the difference between anti-corrosion paint and an epoxy coating?
They are different coating systems with different preparation, adhesion and resistance requirements. We specify the primer and finish according to the metal, operating temperature, exposure and condition found during the inspection.
